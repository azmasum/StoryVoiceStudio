[SCENE: Platform 9]

The last train home was always late, and Maya always waited for it anyway.

It was 11:47 on a Tuesday night in November 2023, and the station smelled like rain and old newspapers. She counted the tiles under her feet — 214 of them between the bench and the yellow line — because counting kept the panic quiet.

$12.50 sat folded in her jacket pocket. It was everything she had until Friday.

"Station closes at midnight," the old attendant said, not looking up from his magazine.

[PAUSE:1]

"I know," Maya whispered. [EMOTION:FEAR] But the trains had stopped announcing destinations an hour ago, and nobody seemed to notice but her.

[SCENE: The Announcement]

At 11:52, the speakers crackled to life.

"Now arriving: Track 9... the last train home."

Maya looked up. The board said the train wouldn't arrive for another 6 minutes, but she could already feel it — a low hum in the rails, a shiver in the concrete. That was wrong. Trains didn't hum before they arrived. Trains didn't make the ground feel nervous.

A man in a gray coat stepped onto the platform beside her. She hadn't heard him walk down the stairs. Nobody ever did, she realized later.

"You're waiting for it too?" he asked quietly.

"It's not due yet," she said.

The man smiled without turning his head. [WHISPER] "It's been 'not due' since 1998."

[SCENE: Track 9]

The lights at the end of the tunnel turned from red to white.

Maya thought about the stories her grandmother used to tell, back in their little house on Ashford Street — about a train that came when the night got too heavy, that collected whoever was tired enough to stop fighting. Her grandmother had died waiting for something. Maya never asked what.

The hum grew louder. 11:58.

"Whatever you left behind," the man said, stepping backward into the dark, "it will still be there tomorrow. That's the trick of it. That's always the trick."

Headlights bloomed in the tunnel — soft, warm, wrong. There was no sound of brakes. There was no sound at all now, except her own heart counting out the seconds like falling coins.

[PAUSE:2]

The doors opened.

Inside, every seat was taken by people who stared straight ahead — tired people, patient people, people holding flowers wrapped in yesterday's news.

Maya stood at the yellow line with her $12.50 and her whole life in one small bag, and she made the choice nobody in her family had ever managed to make.

"No," she said. "Not tonight."

She stepped back as the doors closed. Through the window, the gray-coated man pressed his hand against the glass — and for just a second, he looked relieved.

[SCENE: Wednesday]

The train pulled away without a whisper, and the station lights buzzed back on like nothing happened.

Maya walked home in the dark, 32 blocks through the cold. When she finally reached her apartment, she slept until noon — deeper than she had in years.

Some people wait their whole lives for something to save them.

Maya learned, on the last Tuesday in November, that saving yourself is also a way home.
